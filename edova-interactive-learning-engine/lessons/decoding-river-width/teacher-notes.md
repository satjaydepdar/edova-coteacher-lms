# Teacher Notes — Decoding River Width

## Teaching Tips

- **Draw it live.** Before or after the video, redraw triangle ABC on the
  board from scratch while narrating each label out loud ("A is where I'm
  standing… C is directly across… the right angle is at A because the
  river bank is straight…"). Students retain the labelling far better when
  they see it built stroke-by-stroke rather than shown finished.
- **Use the classroom as the riverbank.** If space allows, mark a baseline
  with tape on the classroom floor and have students sight a fixed object
  (a poster, a door handle) across the room using a protractor held at eye
  level. A physical baseline makes "angle of sight" concrete instead of
  abstract.
- **Anchor the special angles.** Before this lesson, make sure students can
  produce `tan(30°) = 1/√3`, `tan(45°) = 1`, `tan(60°) = √3` from memory or
  a ratio table without hesitation — this lesson assumes that recall speed
  and does not re-teach it.
- **Slow down at the rearrangement step (concept 4).** The jump from
  `tan(B) = AC/AB` to `AC = AB × tan(B)` is often where students lose the
  thread, even though it is "just algebra." Consider pausing the video
  yourself at `03:45` and asking a student to explain the rearrangement in
  their own words before continuing.
- **Make the verification step (concept 6) the emotional payoff.** Frame it
  as "how do we know we're not wrong?" rather than as an extra chore — this
  is what separates a memorized formula from real scientific reasoning, and
  it is the part of the lesson most likely to stick.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"You need to cross the river to measure it."** Students sometimes
   assume some form of direct measurement is still required. Emphasize
   explicitly that every quantity used in the final answer (baseline,
   angle) is measured entirely from the near bank.
2. **Right angle placed at the wrong vertex.** The most frequent labelling
   error is placing the right angle at B instead of A. Remind students the
   right angle exists because the observer stood *directly opposite* the
   landmark — that "directly opposite" relationship is what creates the
   90° angle at A, not at the point they walked to.
3. **Using the wrong ratio (sine or cosine instead of tangent).** Because
   this chapter follows lessons on all three ratios, students may
   default to whichever ratio they used most recently rather than checking
   which sides are actually known. Reinforce the rule: no hypotenuse is
   known or needed in this problem, which immediately rules out sine and
   cosine.
4. **Treating a small mismatch in verification as total failure.** When the
   predicted and measured verification angles differ by a degree or two,
   some students conclude "the method doesn't work." Use this as a teaching
   moment about measurement tolerance versus a genuine error.

## Alternative Explanations

- **Similar triangles framing (no trigonometry required).** For students
  who have not yet covered trigonometric ratios, the same problem can be
  solved using similar triangles: sight the far tree using a handheld
  right-angled object (e.g., a set square) at a known height and distance
  from the eye, and use the ratio of similar triangles instead of `tan`.
  This is a useful bridge for classes revisiting the concept before formal
  trigonometry is introduced.
- **Shadow-and-stick analogy.** For students who struggle with the
  riverbank scenario specifically, relate it back to the shadow-height
  problems typically taught earlier in the chapter: the baseline here plays
  the same role as the shadow length, and the angle of sight plays the same
  role as the sun's angle of elevation. The underlying triangle and formula
  are identical.
- **Google Maps sanity check.** For a quick real-world hook, pull up a
  satellite image of a real river near the school and ask students to
  estimate its width visually before the lesson, then compare their guess
  to what the tangent-ratio method would produce with plausible baseline
  and angle values.

## Discussion Points

- "Why does this method work on a river but not on, say, the width of a
  lake with an irregular, curving shore?" (Discuss the requirement that AC
  be perpendicular to the baseline — i.e., that the observer can identify a
  point "directly opposite" the target.)
- "What real jobs use exactly this technique today?" (Land surveyors, civil
  engineers planning bridges, GIS/cartography, military reconnaissance,
  drone-based mapping.)
- "If you only had a smartphone and no clinometer, how might you
  approximate the angle of sight?" (Compass/level apps, a protractor held
  against the phone screen, or an AR measuring app — good springboard into
  discussing instrument precision.)
- "What would happen to our confidence in the answer if the two
  verification angles matched exactly to the decimal place?" (Prompt
  students to be skeptical of suspiciously perfect real-world data — small
  discrepancies are actually a sign of an honest measurement.)

## Assessment Suggestions

- Use `q6` and `q7` from `questions.md` as a short two-question exit ticket
  to separately assess procedural fluency (q6) and evaluative reasoning
  (q7).
- For a practical assessment, take students outside (or to a large hall)
  and have them measure the "width" of an inaccessible feature (a flower
  bed, a marked-off area) using a real baseline and a protractor, then
  verify with a second triangle exactly as in concept 6.
- For higher-Bloom assessment (Create), assign the transfer task implied by
  `lo11`: have students design — but not necessarily execute — a full
  measurement plan for finding the height of the school's flagpole or
  building, specifying where they would place the baseline and which angle
  they would measure.

**Scene 1 — Hook (`00:00`–`00:20`)**

[00:00] A river cuts through a valley, wide and fast-flowing, with no
bridge in sight.
[00:04] Two hundred years ago, a surveyor mapping this land needed to know
exactly how wide it was — without ever stepping into the water.
[00:11] How do you measure something you cannot walk across, wrap a tape
around, or touch?
[00:16] Today, we decode the river's width using nothing but angles, a
measuring tape on dry land, and one powerful idea from trigonometry.

**Scene 2 — Concept c1: The Indirect Measurement Problem (`00:20`–`00:55`)**

[00:22] This is the problem of indirect measurement — finding a distance
you cannot access directly.
[00:28] Surveyors, cartographers, and engineers have faced this problem for
centuries: measuring mountains, canyons, and rivers from a safe, reachable
position.
[00:37] The tool that solves it is the same one you have already met — the
right triangle, and the ratios hidden inside it.
[00:45] If we can turn the far bank of the river into the corner of a
triangle, and the near bank into its base, the width becomes a side we can
calculate instead of a side we have to walk.
[00:54] That is the core idea of this lesson.

**Scene 3 — Concept c2: Angle of Elevation and Line of Sight (`00:55`–`01:40`)**

[00:57] Concept two: the angle of elevation and the line of sight.
[01:03] Imagine standing on the near bank, directly facing a large tree on
the far bank.
[01:09] The straight line from your eye to the base of that tree is called
the line of sight.
[01:15] When you turn your line of sight to look along the riverbank
instead — say, toward a second point you have marked — the angle between
that new direction and your original line of sight is what surveyors
measure with an instrument called a clinometer or theodolite.
[01:30] This measured angle is the single most important number in our
entire calculation.
[01:35] Get the angle right, and the rest is just arithmetic.

**Scene 5 — Concept c3: Constructing the Right Triangle (`01:55`–`02:50`)**

[01:57] Concept three: setting up the right triangle.
[02:03] Let point A be where you stand on the near bank, directly opposite
a tree at point C on the far bank.
[02:10] Because A is directly opposite C, the line AC — the river's width —
meets the riverbank at a perfect right angle.
[02:18] Now walk along your own bank, in a straight line, to a second point
B, and measure that distance carefully — this is called the baseline.
[02:27] From point B, sight the same tree at C and measure the angle
between your baseline and that line of sight — angle B.
[02:35] You now have a right triangle, ABC, right-angled at A, with one
known side — the baseline AB — and one known angle — angle B.
[02:44] Everything else in that triangle can now be found using
trigonometry.

**Scene 7 — Concept c4: The Tangent Ratio (`03:10`–`04:10`)**

[03:12] Concept four: the tangent ratio.
[03:17] In any right triangle, the tangent of an angle equals the length of
the side opposite that angle divided by the length of the side adjacent to
it.
[03:26] In triangle ABC, the side opposite angle B is AC — the river width
we want.
[03:33] The side adjacent to angle B is AB — our baseline, which we already
measured on dry land.
[03:40] So tan of angle B equals AC divided by AB.
[03:45] Rearranging that single equation gives us AC equals AB multiplied
by tan of angle B.
[03:52] Notice what just happened — an unreachable distance across a river
has become a simple multiplication.

**Scene 9 — Concept c5: Solving for River Width (`04:30`–`05:30`)**

[04:32] Concept five: solving a real example.
[04:37] Suppose a surveyor measures a baseline AB of forty metres along the
riverbank.
[04:44] Sighting the tree from point B gives an angle of thirty degrees.
[04:49] Using our formula, AC equals forty metres multiplied by tan of
thirty degrees.
[04:56] Tan of thirty degrees equals one over the square root of three,
which is approximately zero point five seven seven.
[05:04] Forty multiplied by zero point five seven seven gives approximately
twenty three point one metres.
[05:11] That is the width of the river — calculated entirely from dry land,
using one length and one angle.
[05:19] No boat, no rope, no risk — just geometry doing the crossing for
us.

**Scene 11 — Concept c6: Verification via a Second Baseline (`05:50`–`06:50`)**

[05:52] Concept six: verifying the answer.
[05:57] Good surveyors never trust a single measurement — they check it.
[06:03] Walk twenty metres further along the bank to a third point, D, so
the full baseline AD is now sixty metres.
[06:11] Measure the new angle of sight to the same tree — angle ADC.
[06:16] If our first answer of twenty three point one metres is correct,
this new angle should be close to twenty one degrees, because tan inverse
of twenty three point one over sixty is approximately twenty one degrees.
[06:29] If the measured angle matches the predicted angle, the width
calculation is confirmed.
[06:35] If it does not match, it is a signal to re-check the baseline
distance or the angle readings before trusting the result.
[06:43] This second triangle, sharing the same side AC, is what makes the
method self-checking.

# Art Integration

Art-integrated activities for Coordinate Geometry.

---

## Activity 1: Coordinate Picture Art — The Textbook Figure

**Time:** 30 minutes | **Group Size:** Individual | **Materials:** Graph paper (1 cm grid), fine-tip pen, coloured pencils

### Context
The textbook's opening exercise asks you to plot a series of points and join them to reveal a picture. This activity extends that into a full art piece.

### Procedure
1. On graph paper, draw x and y axes covering at least −1 to 10 on both axes.
2. Carefully plot every point from the textbook exercise:
   - A(4, 8), B(3, 9), C(3, 8), D(1, 6), E(1, 5), F(3, 3), G(6, 3), H(8, 5), I(8, 6), J(6, 8), K(6, 9), L(5, 8)
   - Triangles: PQR with P(3.5, 7), Q(3, 6), R(4, 6); XYZ with X(5.5, 7), Y(5, 6), Z(6, 6); STU with S(4, 5), T(4.5, 4), U(5, 5)
   - Extensions to (0, 5), (0, 6), (9, 5), (9, 6)
3. Join all points as directed. Identify the figure.
4. **Art Extension:** Colour the figure creatively. Add a background scene (a landscape, a room, an abstract setting) using only straight lines whose endpoints have integer or half-integer coordinates.
5. Write down 5 additional coordinate pairs you used for your background and what they represent.

### Learning Outcome
Students see that precise coordinate plotting can produce recognisable, artistic figures. Fractional coordinates gain meaning when they shape real images.

## Activity 2: String Art on a Coordinate Grid

**Time:** 45 minutes | **Group Size:** Pairs | **Materials:** Corkboard, push pins, coloured thread, printed coordinate grid overlay, ruler

### Context
String art creates curves from straight lines — a visual demonstration that many points (each on a different line segment) can collectively form a smooth shape. This connects to the idea that a locus is a set of points satisfying a condition.

### Procedure
1. Pin a printed coordinate grid (0 to 20 on both axes) onto a corkboard.
2. **Phase 1 — Line segments:** Pin thread along the segment from (0, 0) to (20, 10). Then pin from (0, 2) to (18, 10). Continue: from (0, 4) to (16, 10), then (0, 6) to (14, 10), and so on, increasing the y-start by 2 and decreasing the x-end by 2.
3. Observe the curve that emerges from these straight lines.
4. **Phase 2 — Calculate:** For each line segment, use the distance formula to calculate its length. Record in a table:

| Segment | Start | End | Length (by formula) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | (0, 0) | (20, 10) | |
| 2 | (0, 2) | (18, 10) | |
| 3 | (0, 4) | (16, 10) | |
| ... | ... | ... | |

5. **Phase 3 — Midpoints:** Find and mark the midpoint of each line segment using the midpoint formula. What pattern do these midpoints follow? Plot them and join — do they form a curve?
6. **Phase 4 — Creative:** Design your own string art pattern using at least 8 line segments. Write the coordinates of each segment's endpoints. Calculate the length and midpoint of each.

### Reflection
- How does string art relate to the concept of a locus?
- If you used the section formula instead of just the midpoint (ratio 1:1), how could you create a different pattern?

### Learning Outcome
Students experience coordinate geometry as a generative art tool and see how midpoints and distances create emergent visual patterns.

## Activity 3: Geometric Pattern Design Using the Distance Formula

**Time:** 35 minutes | **Group Size:** Individual | **Materials:** Graph paper, compass, ruler, coloured pencils

### Context
Many traditional geometric patterns (Islamic tessellations, kolam designs, Celtic knots) rely on precise equal spacing and symmetry. The distance formula can be used to verify and construct these patterns.

### Procedure
1. On graph paper, mark a centre point O at (10, 10).
2. Place 8 points on a circle of radius 5 units around O. Use the distance formula to verify each point is exactly 5 units from O:
   - P₁(15, 10), P₂(13.54, 13.54), P₃(10, 15), P₄(6.46, 13.54), P₅(5, 10), P₆(6.46, 6.46), P₇(10, 5), P₈(13.54, 6.46)
3. Verify: distance of each Pᵢ from O = √[(xᵢ − 10)² + (yᵢ − 10)²] = 5.
4. Connect every alternate point (P₁ to P₃, P₃ to P₅, etc.) to form an octagram (star polygon).
5. Verify that the sides of this octagram are all equal using the distance formula.
6. **Art Extension:** Fill alternating regions with two contrasting colours. Add a second, smaller octagram inside using points at radius 3. Verify those points are 3 units from O.

### Worksheet Verification Table

| Point | x | y | Distance from O(10,10) | Expected | Match? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P₁ | 15 | 10 | | 5 | |
| P₂ | 13.54 | 13.54 | | 5 | |
| P₃ | 10 | 15 | | 5 | |
| P₄ | 6.46 | 13.54 | | 5 | |
| P₅ | 5 | 10 | | 5 | |
| P₆ | 6.46 | 6.46 | | 5 | |
| P₇ | 10 | 5 | | 5 | |
| P₈ | 13.54 | 6.46 | | 5 | |

### Learning Outcome
Students use the distance formula as a precision tool in art construction, understanding its role in creating regular polygons and symmetric designs.

## Activity 4: Reflection Symmetry Art

**Time:** 30 minutes | **Group Size:** Individual | **Materials:** Graph paper, coloured pencils

### Context
Reflection across the x-axis, y-axis, or the line y = x transforms (x, y) to (−x, y), (x, −y), or (y, x) respectively. These transformations are fundamental to creating symmetric art.

### Procedure
1. In the first quadrant, draw a simple freehand shape using at least 8 coordinate points. Record each point.
   Example: (2, 1), (4, 1), (5, 3), (5, 6), (4, 8), (2, 8), (1, 6), (1, 3)
2. **Reflection 1 — y-axis:** Transform each point to (−x, y). Plot and join to create a mirror image in the second quadrant. Colour both shapes in the same colour.
3. **Reflection 2 — x-axis:** Take your original shape and transform each point to (x, −y). Plot in the fourth quadrant. Colour in a different colour.
4. **Reflection 3 — Origin:** Transform to (−x, −y). Plot in the third quadrant. Use a third colour.
5. **Verification:** Pick any two corresponding reflected points and use the distance formula to verify they are equidistant from the axis/origin of reflection. For example, (5, 3) and (−5, 3) should be equidistant from the y-axis (x = 0).
6. **Art Extension:** Make the original shape an asymmetric "creature" or "abstract form." The four reflections together create a mandala-like composition. Add inner details and colour to complete the artwork.

### Verification Table

| Original Point | Reflected Point | Axis/Origin | Distance of Original from Axis | Distance of Reflection from Axis | Equal? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (5, 3) | (−5, 3) | y-axis | | | |
| (4, 8) | (4, −8) | x-axis | | | |
| (2, 1) | (−2, −1) | Origin | | | |

### Learning Outcome
Students internalise coordinate reflection rules through creative practice and verify symmetry algebraically using the distance formula.

## Activity 5: Mandala Design Using the Section Formula

**Time:** 40 minutes | **Group Size:** Pairs | **Materials:** Graph paper, compass, ruler, coloured pencils or fine pens

### Context
Mandalas are built from concentric rings of evenly spaced elements. The section formula can determine the exact coordinates of decorative elements placed along radial lines at specific fractional distances.

### Procedure
1. Draw axes on graph paper with centre at (0, 0).
2. Draw 6 radial lines from the origin at 60° intervals. The endpoints on a circle of radius 12 are:
   - (12, 0), (6, 10.39), (−6, 10.39), (−12, 0), (−6, −10.39), (6, −10.39)
3. **Ring 1 (radius 4):** Use the section formula to find points at 1/3 of the distance from origin to each endpoint. The origin is (0,0), so the section formula gives:
   - x = (1 × x_end + 2 × 0) / (1 + 2) = x_end / 3
   - y = (1 × y_end + 2 × 0) / (1 + 2) = y_end / 3
   - Calculate all 6 Ring 1 points.
4. **Ring 2 (radius 8):** Find points at 2/3 of the distance from origin to each endpoint using the same logic:
   - x = (2 × x_end + 1 × 0) / (2 + 1) = 2 × x_end / 3
   - y = (2 × y_end + 1 × 0) / (2 + 1) = 2 × y_end / 3
   - Calculate all 6 Ring 2 points.
5. At each Ring 1 point, draw a small diamond shape (4 coordinate points). At each Ring 2 point, draw a small circle.
6. **Verification:** Use the distance formula to confirm that all Ring 1 points are exactly 4 units from the origin, and all Ring 2 points are exactly 8 units from the origin.
7. **Connect the rings:** Draw hexagons by joining adjacent points within each ring. Verify that all sides of each hexagon are equal using the distance formula.
8. Colour your mandala using a consistent colour scheme (e.g., warm colours for Ring 1, cool colours for Ring 2).

### Calculation Table

| Radial Endpoint | Ring 1 Point (1/3) | Ring 1 Dist from O | Ring 2 Point (2/3) | Ring 2 Dist from O |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (12, 0) | | | | |
| (6, 10.39) | | | | |
| (−6, 10.39) | | | | |
| (−12, 0) | | | | |
| (−6, −10.39) | | | | |
| (6, −10.39) | | | | |

### Reflection
- How does the section formula make mandala construction more precise than freehand drawing?
- What ratio would you use to add a Ring 3 at radius 10?
- What happens if you use different ratios for different radial lines? (This breaks symmetry — discuss why mandalas require uniform ratios.)

### Learning Outcome
The section formula becomes a design precision tool. Students see fractions and ratios as artistic parameters that control the structure of a composition.

## Activity 6: Perspective Grid Drawing

**Time:** 35 minutes | **Group Size:** Individual | **Materials:** Graph paper, ruler, pencil, coloured pencils

### Context
Perspective drawing uses a vanishing point. On a coordinate grid, a vanishing point can be placed at a specific coordinate, and lines drawn from it to points on a "ground line" create the illusion of depth — this is a visual application of collinearity.

### Procedure
1. Set up a coordinate grid. Place the ground line along y = 0, from x = −10 to x = 10.
2. Place a vanishing point at V(0, 15).
3. Mark equally spaced points on the ground line: (−8, 0), (−6, 0), (−4, 0), (−2, 0), (0, 0), (2, 0), (4, 0), (6, 0), (8, 0).
4. Draw lines from V to each ground point. These are your perspective lines.
5. Draw a horizontal line at y = 5 (representing a "back wall"). Mark where each perspective line crosses this line.
6. **Collinearity check:** For each perspective line, verify using the distance formula that the vanishing point, the ground point, and the intersection point are collinear (i.e., the sum of two segment lengths equals the third).

| Ground Point | Intersection at y=5 | V to Ground | Ground to Intersection | V to Intersection | Collinear? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (−8, 0) | | | | | |
| (−6, 0) | | | | | |
| (−4, 0) | | | | | |
| (0, 0) | | | | | |
| (4, 0) | | | | | |
| (8, 0) | | | | | |

7. **Art Extension:** Between adjacent perspective lines at y = 5, draw rectangles to create the illusion of 3D buildings or rooms. Add doors, windows, and rooftops. Colour to enhance the depth illusion — objects closer to the vanishing point should appear smaller and lighter.

### Connection to the Textbook
In Exercise 7.1 Q3, you checked collinearity of (1, 5), (2, 3), and (−2, −11) by verifying AB + BC = AC. Here you do the same, but the collinear points create the visual structure of a perspective drawing.

### Learning Outcome
Students see collinearity as the mathematical principle behind perspective drawing — connecting Renaissance art to coordinate geometry.

## Activity 7: Distance-Based Colour Mapping (Heat Map Art)

**Time:** 30 minutes | **Group Size:** Individual | **Materials:** Graph paper, set of coloured pencils (at least 6 colours), ruler

### Context
In data visualisation and digital art, the distance of a point from a reference point often determines its colour (heat maps, elevation maps, thermal imaging). This activity applies the distance formula to create a colour-field artwork.

### Procedure
1. On graph paper (20 × 20 grid, coordinates from 0 to 19 on both axes), mark a "heat source" point at H(10, 10).
2. Assign colours based on distance from H:
   - Distance 0–2: **Red** (hottest)
   - Distance 2–4: **Orange**
   - Distance 4–6: **Yellow**
   - Distance 6–8: **Green**
   - Distance 8–10: **Blue**
   - Distance > 10: **Leave blank** or use very light grey
3. For every integer-coordinate point (x, y) on your grid, calculate its distance from H using the distance formula: d = √[(x − 10)² + (y − 10)²].
4. Colour each grid cell according to its distance band.
5. **Observe the pattern:** The coloured region should form a circle of radius 10 centred at H, with concentric colour rings inside it.

### Verification Questions
- The boundary between Red and Orange zones: what is its shape? Prove algebraically that it is part of a circle centred at H with radius 2. (Every point on this boundary satisfies √[(x−10)² + (y−10)²] = 2, i.e., (x−10)² + (y−10)² = 4.)
- Similarly, prove each colour boundary is a circle of radius 4, 6, 8, and 10.

### Variation — Two Heat Sources
Place two heat sources at H₁(5, 5) and H₂(15, 15). For each point, calculate distances to both. Colour based on the **minimum** of the two distances. Observe the interference pattern where the two "heat zones" meet — this is a Voronoi diagram, used in city planning, biology, and robotics.

### Connection to the Textbook
Example 4 asks: "Find a relation between x and y such that the point (x, y) is equidistant from (7, 1) and (3, 5)." The answer, x − y = 2, is the boundary line in a two-source heat map where both sources are equally "hot." This activity makes that abstract concept visually tangible.

### Learning Outcome
Students repeatedly practise the distance formula in a creative context and discover that the locus of points at a fixed distance from a point is a circle — connecting algebra, geometry, and art in one exercise.

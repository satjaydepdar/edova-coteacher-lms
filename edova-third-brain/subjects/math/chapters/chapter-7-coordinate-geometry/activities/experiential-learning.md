## Activity 1: Town Map Distance Calculation

**Time:** 40 minutes | **Setting:** Outdoor / Corridor | **Materials:** Chalk, measuring tape, worksheet

### Real-Life Context
A town B is located 36 km east and 15 km north of town A. How do we find the distance without physically measuring it?

### Procedure
1. On the ground, draw x and y axes using chalk. Choose a scale: 1 m = 3 km (so 12 m = 36 km, 5 m = 15 km).
2. Mark town A at the origin (0, 0).
3. From A, walk 12 m along the x-axis (east) and mark a point. From there, walk 5 m perpendicular (north) and mark town B.
4. Now use a measuring tape to directly measure the distance AB.
5. Calculate using the distance formula: AB = √(36² + 15²) = √(1296 + 225) = √1521 = 39 km.
6. Convert back using the scale: 39 km ÷ 3 = 13 m. Compare with the measured value.

### Reflection Questions
- Why is the calculated distance the same as the measured distance?
- In what real-world situations would you need this method instead of direct measurement?
- How does the Pythagorean Theorem connect to this?

### Learning Outcome
Students experience that coordinate geometry is not just paper-based — it models real spatial relationships. The scale conversion deepens understanding of proportional reasoning.

## Activity 2: Classroom Seating — Are They in a Line?

**Time:** 15 minutes | **Setting:** Inside classroom | **Materials:** Seating chart

### Real-Life Context
In a classroom, Ashima is at A(3, 1), Bharti at B(6, 4), and Camella at C(8, 6). Are they seated in a straight line?

### Procedure
1. Create a coordinate grid of your classroom: rows = y-axis, columns = x-axis.
2. Identify the positions of three of your classmates and note their coordinates.
3. Calculate the three pairwise distances using the distance formula.
4. Check if AB + BC = AC (or any permutation).
5. If collinear, physically verify by stretching a string between the outer two students — does the middle student lie on the string?
6. If not collinear, measure the angle at the middle student using a protractor. Does it match what the converse of Pythagoras Theorem predicts?

### Extension
- Find the equation of the line on which the collinear students sit.
- Predict: if a fourth student sits at (10, 8), will they also be on this line? Verify.

### Learning Outcome
Transforms an abstract collinearity problem into a physically verifiable classroom experiment.

## Activity 3: Sports Day Flag Placement on Ground Grid

**Time:** 45 minutes | **Setting:** School ground | **Materials:** Chalk powder, flag sticks (green, red, blue), measuring tape

### Real-Life Context
A rectangular school ground ABCD has chalk lines drawn at 1 m intervals. Flower pots are placed at 1 m intervals along AD. Niharika runs ¼th of AD on the 2nd line and posts a green flag. Preet runs ⅕th of AD on the 8th line and posts a red flag.

### Procedure
1. On the school ground, mark a rectangle of convenient size (e.g., 20 m × 10 m).
2. Draw parallel lines at 1 m intervals along the length.
3. Along the base (AD), mark points at every 1 m.
4. **Green flag:** On the 2nd line, measure ¼ of AD from A. Place the flag. Note its coordinates.
5. **Red flag:** On the 8th line, measure ⅕ of AD from A. Place the flag. Note its coordinates.
6. Calculate the distance between the two flags using the distance formula.
7. **Blue flag:** Find the midpoint of the segment joining the green and red flags. Place the blue flag there.
8. Measure the actual distance between green and red flags with tape. Compare with calculated distance.

### Worksheet Questions
- If AD = 20 m, what are the exact coordinates of all three flags?
- What is the distance between the green and red flags?
- What are the coordinates of the blue flag?
- If Rashmi wanted to place a yellow flag at the point of trisection closer to the green flag, where would it be?

### Learning Outcome
Students physically enact the section formula and distance formula on a large-scale grid, bridging the gap between small graph-paper coordinates and real-world measurements.

## Activity 4: GPS-Inspired Coordinate Treasure Hunt

**Time:** 50 minutes | **Setting:** School campus | **Materials:** Coordinate clues cards, compass (optional)

### Real-Life Context
GPS navigation systems use coordinates to locate places and calculate distances. In this activity, students act as "human GPS" units.

### Setup
The teacher hides 5 "treasures" at known locations around the school campus. Each location is described by coordinates on a pre-drawn map of the school.

### Procedure
1. Each team receives a school map with a coordinate grid overlaid.
2. **Clue 1:** "Start at the school gate at (0, 0). The first treasure is at (12, 5). Calculate the distance and walk in the right direction."
3. At each treasure location, teams find the next coordinate clue.
4. **Clue 2 example:** "From here, walk to the point that is equidistant from (20, 0) and (20, 10). The treasure is buried there."
5. **Clue 3 example:** "The next treasure is at the midpoint of the segment joining the library (30, 8) and the canteen (30, 16)."
6. **Clue 4 example:** "Find the point on the x-axis that is equidistant from your current position and the principal's office."
7. The first team to collect all 5 treasures wins.

### Debrief
- Which clue was the hardest? Why?
- How did knowing the distance formula help you estimate walking distance?
- In what ways is this similar to how Google Maps works?

### Learning Outcome
Students experience coordinate geometry as a navigation tool, understanding its real-world application in GPS and mapping systems.

## Activity 5: Perpendicular Bisector Discovery with Paper Folding

**Time:** 20 minutes | **Setting:** Classroom | **Materials:** Wax paper or tracing paper, ruler, pen

### Real-Life Context
The set of all points equidistant from two given points forms the perpendicular bisector. This principle is used in earthquake seismology, radar systems, and cellular tower placement.

### Procedure
1. On a sheet of paper, mark two points A and B, 8 cm apart.
2. Fold the paper so that point A exactly coincides with point B. Crease the fold sharply.
3. Unfold and observe the crease line. Mark 5–6 points on this crease.
4. Measure the distance from each marked point to A and to B. Record in a table.

| Point on crease | Distance to A | Distance to B |
|---|---|---|
| P₁ | | |
| P₂ | | |
| P₃ | | |
| P₄ | | |
| P₅ | | |

5. **Algebraic verification:** Assign coordinates A(7, 1) and B(3, 5). Show that the equation of the crease (perpendicular bisector) is x − y = 2.
6. **Connection:** Verify that the point (0, 9) lies on this line and is equidistant from A and B (as in the textbook example).

### Reflection
- Why does the folding method produce the perpendicular bisector?
- What happens if A and B are on the same horizontal line?
- How is this used in finding the centre of a circle?

### Learning Outcome
A tactile, geometry-first approach that is then connected back to the algebraic formulation — reinforcing the coordinate geometry bridge between algebra and geometry.

## Activity 6: Architectural Floor Plan — Coordinate Modelling

**Time:** 60 minutes (can be split across two periods) | **Setting:** Classroom | **Materials:** Graph paper, ruler, colour pencils

### Real-Life Context
Architects and engineers use coordinate geometry to design floor plans, position pillars, and ensure structural symmetry. In this activity, students design a small apartment floor plan and verify geometric properties.

### Procedure
1. On graph paper, draw x and y axes. Each unit = 1 metre.
2. Design a rectangular room with vertices at (2, 2), (10, 2), (10, 8), (2, 8).
3. **Task A — Door placement:** A door is to be placed at the midpoint of the wall from (2, 2) to (2, 8). Find the coordinates of the door.
4. **Task B — Window placement:** Windows are to be placed at the points of trisection of the wall from (10, 2) to (10, 8). Find the coordinates.
5. **Task C — Pillar positioning:** Four pillars are at the vertices. Verify that they form a rectangle using the distance formula (check: opposite sides equal, diagonals equal).
6. **Task D — Furniture:** Place a bed (rectangle) with vertices at (3, 3), (7, 3), (7, 6), (3, 6). Verify it is a rectangle.
7. **Task E — Diagonal wiring:** An electrician needs to run a wire from (2, 2) to (10, 8). Calculate the length of wire needed.
8. **Task F — Centre point:** Find the point where the two diagonals of the room intersect. What property does this point satisfy?

### Extension Challenge
- Add a triangular balcony with vertices (10, 8), (14, 8), (10, 12). Calculate its area using the coordinate geometry area formula.
- Place a circular table of radius 1 m centred at the intersection of the room diagonals. Will it fit without overlapping the bed?

### Learning Outcome
Students apply distance formula, section formula, midpoint formula, and diagonal properties in a unified, purposeful design task — mirroring how these tools are used in architecture and engineering.

## Activity 7: Relay Tower Positioning Problem

**Time:** 25 minutes | **Setting:** Classroom | **Materials:** Worksheet, calculator

### Real-Life Context
A telephone company wants to position a relay tower between towns A and B such that the tower is twice as far from B as from A. This is the exact scenario from Section 7.3 of the textbook.

### Procedure
1. Town A is at origin (0, 0). Town B is at (36, 15). Scale: 1 unit = 1 km.
2. The tower P divides AB in the ratio AP : PB = 1 : 2.
3. **Step 1:** Use the section formula to find P's coordinates.
   - x = (1×36 + 2×0) / (1+2) = 12
   - y = (1×15 + 2×0) / (1+2) = 5
   - P = (12, 5)
4. **Step 2:** Verify: calculate AP and PB using the distance formula and confirm AP : PB = 1 : 2.
5. **Step 3:** On graph paper, plot A, B, and P. Draw the line segment and verify visually that P is closer to A.
6. **Variation:** If the company now wants a second tower Q that divides AB in the ratio 2 : 1 (closer to B), find Q's coordinates.
7. **Extension:** What is the distance between the two towers P and Q?

### Reflection
- Why would a company want towers at different positions along a route?
- What would happen if the ratio were 1 : 1? Where would the tower be?
- Research: How do mobile networks use coordinate geometry for tower placement?

### Learning Outcome
Students see the section formula as a practical tool for resource positioning, not just an abstract mathematical exercise.

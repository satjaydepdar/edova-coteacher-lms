## Dialogue 1 — Motivating the distance formula

**Teacher:** I want to find the distance between A(4, 0) and B(6, 0). What is it?

**Student:** Two units. Six minus four.

**Teacher:** Good. Now C(0, 3) and D(0, 8)?

**Student:** Five units.

**Teacher:** Now A(4, 0) and C(0, 3)?

**Student:** I can't just subtract because they're on different axes.

**Teacher:** But look at triangle AOC. What kind of triangle is it?

**Student:** Right triangle! OA is 4, OC is 3.

**Teacher:** So how do we find AC?

**Student:** Pythagoras! AC = 5.

**Teacher:** Now what if the points aren't on the axes at all — say P(4, 6) and Q(6, 8)?

**Student:** [thinking] We can still build a right triangle...

**Teacher:** Exactly. That's where the distance formula comes from.

## Dialogue 2 — Addressing the m₁/m₂ confusion

**Teacher:** A(2, 3) and B(8, 7). P divides AB in ratio 2:3. Is P closer to A or B?

**Student:** 2 is smaller, so closer to A.

**Teacher:** Right — AP is 2 parts, PB is 3 parts. Now in the formula, which point's coordinates get the larger multiplier?

**Student:** B should get more weight. But m₁ = 2 goes with x₂...

**Teacher:** Let's check. m₂ = 3 is the LARGER multiplier. It goes with x₁ = 2, which is A. But we said P is closer to A, meaning B should have more weight. Something seems wrong?

**Student:** Wait — let me think again. P is closer to A, so from P's perspective, B is farther away. The ratio AP:PB = 2:3 means the segment near A is shorter. So the formula weights B more because B is farther. That actually makes sense!

**Teacher:** Perfect. The larger multiplier goes with the farther point.

## Dialogue 3 — Discovering the midpoint formula

**Teacher:** If P divides AB in the ratio 1:1, what does that mean geometrically?

**Student:** P is the midpoint.

**Teacher:** So plug m₁ = 1, m₂ = 1 into the section formula. What do you get?

**Student:** x = (1·x₂ + 1·x₁)/(1+1) = (x₁ + x₂)/2. Same for y.

**Teacher:** So the midpoint formula is just...?

**Student:** Just the section formula with equal ratio. I don't need to memorise it separately!

**Teacher:** Exactly. One formula, three uses.

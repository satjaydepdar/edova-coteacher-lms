## For visually impaired students

- Use raised-line graph sheets or tactile coordinate grids
- Verbalise all diagrams: "Point A is 4 units right and 3 units up from the origin"
- Focus on the algebraic process rather than visual verification
- Use larger print materials with high contrast
- Pair with a sighted peer for diagram-dependent problems
- Emphasise that the formulas work algebraically without needing to see the picture

## For students with dyscalculia

- Provide formula strips that can be physically placed on the desk
- Use colour coding consistently: one colour for x-values, another for y-values
- Break multi-step problems into single-step cards that students arrange in order
- Allow calculator use for arithmetic (the focus is on the method, not computation)
- Provide worked examples with every step shown — reduce cognitive load of "what do I do next?"

## For ELL students

- Create a vocabulary sheet with: abscissa, ordinate, quadrant, collinear, perpendicular, bisect, trisection
- Use visual dictionaries — each term with a diagram
- Allow bilingual resources for the derivation (understanding the logic matters more than the language)
- Provide sentence frames: "The distance between A and B is calculated by..." "Point P divides AB in the ratio..."

## For gifted students

- See differentiation section (Tier 3 strategies)
- Offer independent exploration projects: "How would you find the area of any polygon given its vertices?"

## General principles

- Coordinate geometry is inherently visual — always have a fallback for students who cannot access the visual channel
- The algebra is mechanical — separate assessment of "can you set up the problem" from "can you do the arithmetic"
- Group work is particularly effective here: one student plots, one calculates, one verifies
